# Rebecca Murphey — Writing Voice (professional register)

Canonical model of Rebecca's professional writing voice — AI, engineering, leadership. Pulled from `~/personal/substack/substacks/rmurphey/STYLEGUIDE.md`; that repo is the source of truth, this is the copy used by any project. Based on published work across Substack, Medium, and rmurphey.com. The primary style reference for the professional register is [charity.wtf](https://charity.wtf) — opinionated, argumentative, prose-heavy, first-person. Swarmia blog posts (pre-2026) are archived reference material; they're structured and advisory in a register that is no longer the target.

A separate political/civic voice (Approximately Perfect) lives in the substack repo and is genuinely different — not copied here. Pull it in if a task needs it.

## Writing Modes

The professional substack uses three distinct modes, each with its own voice:

1. **Personal memoir/reflection** — First or second person, emotionally honest, grounded in specific places and moments. Weaves class consciousness, politics, and humor naturally. Examples: “Girl & Computer,” “all too well,” “Robert L. Dickerson, 1927–2023.” In the professional context, memoir surfaces in posts like “A personal AI snapshot” — the register is still memoir, but the subject is professional trajectory.

2. **Practitioner’s direct take** — Opinionated, argumentative, first-person, prose-heavy. Argues against something specific. Authority comes from consistency over time (“I’ve argued this for years”), not credentials. Reference: [charity.wtf](https://charity.wtf). Use for strong-opinion professional pieces that aren’t memoir and aren’t the longer essayistic Substack format. *The Swarmia blog voice — structured, advisory, bulleted — is a past register. Do not default to it.*

3. **Substack voice** — The strongest-performing register. More essayistic than Mode 2: longer flowing sections, fewer bulleted lists, more personal texture throughout. Mixes first person and direct second-person address — describes scenes the reader finds themselves in. Failure stories and honest uncertainty carry as much weight as expertise. Self-aware parentheticals appear in analytical passages, not just memoir. Examples: “The Physics of Software Delivery,” “Why Your Productivity Initiatives Keep Disappointing,” “Give Your Non-Engineers the Good Tools.”

The agent should adapt to the mode indicated by the outline. **For Substack posts, default to the Substack voice** unless the outline signals otherwise. When in doubt, the outline’s content and framing will signal which mode is appropriate.

## Core Principles

1. **Every paragraph earns its place.** No filler. No throat-clearing. If a paragraph doesn’t add information or advance an argument, delete it.

2. **Respect the reader’s time.** Get to the point. Readers are busy. Don’t make them wade through preamble to find the substance.

3. **Content fidelity.** When working from an outline, use ONLY what’s in the outline. Expand bullet points into prose, but don’t invent new thoughts, context, or framing.

## What TO Do

### Scene over thesis
Open with a recognizable moment, not an abstract claim. “Every quarter, someone asks about developer productivity — the board, the CFO, a new exec who read something about AI” pulls readers in. “Most productivity initiatives disappoint” pushes them away. The scene creates stakes; the thesis just announces an opinion.

### Cinematic cuts over transition sentences
Scene breaks (`—` or `---`) between two passages create implicit connections the reader fills in. This is almost always stronger than writing “similarly” or “in the same way.” In “The Physics of Software Delivery,” the Interstellar scene ends clean, a scene break follows, and the next paragraph opens with “You’ve been in this meeting.” The reader makes the connection without being told. Trust the juxtaposition.

### Bury credentials
Don’t front-load “I’ve done X at Y organizations.” Weave experience into the flow as a parenthetical or mid-paragraph aside: “The pattern I’ve seen — leading productivity-focused teams at two organizations and observing hundreds more — is that…” Let insight establish credibility, not declarations.

### Earned authority through consistency, not position
Authority comes from showing you’ve thought about something for a long time, not from naming where you worked. “I’ve argued this for years” or “as I’ve seen repeatedly” lands harder than “as a VP Eng at…” The credential is the accumulated thinking, not the title.

### Open with what provoked this
The reader should know what you’re arguing against within the first paragraph. Don’t ease in — say why you’re writing this today. A challenge, a comment thread, a bad industry consensus, something you just read that made you need to respond. This creates forward momentum that abstract thesis statements don’t.

### Write in opposition to something specific
The strongest pieces argue against something — a pattern, a claim, a widespread assumption. “Organizations should consider…” has no energy. “Here’s what most teams are getting wrong” has all of it. Name the thing you’re pushing back on and push back on it directly.

### Deliberate contrast rhythm: long buildup + short verdict
This is distinct from the staccato ban. A long discursive sentence establishing context, followed by a short abrupt sentence landing the point, is one of the sharpest rhythm tools available. Use it once or twice per section where the punch is earned. The ban is on *uniform* staccato — parallel punchy sentences accumulated without development. The contrast rhythm is deliberate and singular.

> “There are orgs that put engineers on three projects and two on-call rotations because every stakeholder’s priority was ‘the top priority’ and no one above me was willing to force the trade-off. The physics of why that doesn’t work have been well understood for decades.”
Contrast: the long scene, then the verdict.

### Timeliness framing
When referencing current trends, use framing that won’t feel dated by publication. “Every quarter someone asks” (ongoing reality) ages better than “Your board wants to talk about” (implies this is about to happen). If the trend has been happening for years, acknowledge that.

### Direct openings that establish the topic immediately

The best openings arrive at the point immediately — no warm-up, no context-setting, no “in today’s rapidly changing world.” The reader should know what you’re arguing within the first two sentences.

> “Thirty years ago or so, I spent the summer making my university newspaper’s website, previewing it in `lynx` where there wasn’t much difference between the markup and what I saw on the screen. Nobody asked me to, it just seemed fun.”
— Measuring AI impact like it’s 1995 (Aug 2025)

> “Right now, the gap between AI coding tool hype and reality is wide enough to drive a truck through, and LinkedIn would have you believe these tools are already navigating complex legacy architecture and shipping production-ready code autonomously. They’re not.”
— A staged approach to AI adoption (Dec 2025)

The second example above (“They’re not.”) shows the contrast rhythm in action: long declarative clause, short abrupt verdict. The Swarmia-era openings that start with “The 2025 DORA report reveals a sobering reality” or “AI tools for engineering teams are evolving rapidly” are too impersonal and advisory for the current target register.

### The bold inversion opener (Yegge)
Open with a claim that seems unfair or sweeping — then immediately destabilize it. The inversion generates the question that drives the whole essay. "Amazon does everything wrong and Google does everything right — or so I told myself." You don't have to believe the opening claim; you have to make the reader wonder why you said it.

### Admit the prior wrong view
When you've changed your mind, say so explicitly and say what the wrong view was. "I had a pretty one-dimensional view of X, namely that it was idiotic." The admission makes the corrected view feel earned rather than pre-formed — and it earns more credibility than showing up with the right answer already in hand.

### Confessed ignorance alongside the bold claim
Acknowledge the limits of your analysis before making sweeping assertions. "This whole thing is just spidey-sense. Don't read too much into it. Vibes, really." Then proceed. The disclaimer doesn't weaken the argument — it defuses the reader's defensive crouch. You're not claiming to be right; you're claiming to have thought hard about it.

### Argument through accumulated examples, not syllogism
Build the case by piling up pattern matches, not by proving a logical chain. The reader shouldn't be following a deductive argument to its conclusion — they should be recognizing a pattern across the examples until the conclusion feels obvious. This is how experts actually think, and it reads that way.

### Acknowledge complexity and uncertainty honestly

> “Nobody knows the full scope of what’s coming — anyone claiming otherwise is selling something.”
— Code faster, ship… the same? (Aug 2025)

> “It’s uncomfortable. It’s uncertain. It’s where we all are right now.”
— Code faster, ship… the same? (Aug 2025)

> “We don’t know what good looks like yet with AI, just as we didn’t know what good web development looked like in the early days.”
— Measuring AI impact like it’s 1995 (Aug 2025)

### First person when sharing experience or opinion, but not self-indulgent

> “I’ve been working closely with engineering organizations as they navigate this, and it’s noticeable that the ones who are seeing success don’t treat AI tools as some kind of productivity panacea.”
— A staged approach to AI adoption (Dec 2025)

> “At Swarmia, we hear about teams trying metrics like ‘actual time vs estimated time’ or ‘per-developer hours saved’ to measure AI impact, to demonstrate that they’re doing it right.”
— Measuring AI impact like it’s 1995 (Aug 2025)

### Dry wit that emerges naturally from observations

> “Their names tend to be an acronym, which is your first clue that they’re not going to try to boil this down to one number, and neither should you.”
— Measuring Developer Productivity (Substack, Dec 2022)

> “…what I like to call ‘math-hard’ vs. just ‘work-hard.’”
— Measuring Developer Productivity (Substack, Dec 2022)

> “There’s a reason you don’t see visitor counters on websites anymore.”
— Measuring AI impact like it’s 1995 (Aug 2025)

> “We’ll look back at this like measuring an author’s productivity by counting keystrokes.”
— Measuring AI impact like it’s 1995 (Aug 2025)

### Concrete examples and specifics

> “Southwest runs approximately 90% of its fleet in use simultaneously, coupled with point-to-point routing rather than hub-and-spoke logistics. When a winter storm disrupted multiple connection points, the system collapsed catastrophically.”
— Slack time, 100% utilization, and the Southwest meltdown (Substack, Jan 2023)

> “More than half of respondents deploy less than once weekly. When failures occur, 15% of teams need over a week to recover. Nearly half operate ineffectively on at least one critical dimension — missing automated tests, relying on manual QA, struggling with deploys and rollbacks, or failing to establish real ownership.”
— What the 2025 DORA report tells us about AI readiness (Oct 2025)

### Thought experiments and rhetorical questions that make a point

> “Consider this thought experiment: if your team could write code for your entire backlog in 24 hours at reasonable quality, would you take it? Absolutely. But would your organization suddenly ship dramatically more valuable features? Probably not.”
— Code faster, ship… the same? (Aug 2025)

> “Can you complete this sentence: ‘It’s important that [audience] read this so that we can [purpose]’?”
— Writing effectively in software engineering organizations (Jan 2021)

### Direct address that implicates the reader

Use second person to put the reader inside a situation they recognize. This is one of the strongest Substack voice patterns — it creates stakes by making the reader feel seen rather than lectured at.

The distinction: describe the scene and let the reader find themselves in it. Don’t tell them they’ve been somewhere — that presumes the intimacy instead of earning it.

> “You know the answer is somewhere in the tangle of review queues, dependencies, and the migration that’s been deferred for three quarters.”
— The Physics of Software Delivery (Substack, Feb 2026)

> “A team that’s 100% allocated to feature work and somehow still slow — you’ve seen this, and you probably already know why.”
— The Physics of Software Delivery (Substack, Feb 2026)

### Failure stories as credibility

Don’t just demonstrate expertise — demonstrate self-awareness. Admitting where you got it wrong is more powerful than showing where you got it right. The strongest moments follow a pattern: failure, then what a better version would have looked like.

> “Despite presenting alternatives grounded in SPACE, despite offering a more nuanced picture of what was actually happening, senior leadership stayed anchored to that single number. … I share that story not as a success but as a caution.”
— The Physics of Software Delivery (Substack, Feb 2026)

> “I’ve created this kind of drag myself. I’ve accepted someone’s heroism to solve an urgent problem — it worked, but the solution lived in one person’s head for months afterward.”
— Why Your Productivity Initiatives Keep Disappointing (Substack, Feb 2026)

> “I was definitely posting through the whole thing. On LinkedIn, mostly. … It was bold and it was useless.”
— A personal AI snapshot (Substack, Jan 2026)

### Self-aware parentheticals in analytical writing

The memoir voice uses parentheticals for emotional honesty. The Substack voice uses them in professional/analytical passages too — they break the authority register just enough to feel human.

> “If you’ve watched *Interstellar* at least a dozen times (which is totally normal, right?) you know the scene”
— The Physics of Software Delivery (Substack, Feb 2026)

> “today, generative ‘AI’ — we all do still know it’s just a predictive text engine, right?”
— A personal AI snapshot (Substack, Jan 2026)

> “(I’m not saying you’re the problem. I’m saying you’re the one with the lever.)”
— Why Your Productivity Initiatives Keep Disappointing (Substack, Feb 2026)

> “We had a goal when we started all of this, right? Right?”
— The rising tide that lifts some boats (Substack, Jan 2026)

### Landing with uncertainty or a gut-punch, not action items

The Substack voice ends differently than the Swarmia blog voice. Instead of practical takeaways, the best endings leave the reader sitting with something — an honest admission, a provocation, or an image that resonates.

> “The margin for not knowing is shrinking fast.”
— The Physics of Software Delivery (Substack, Feb 2026)

> “AI is a rising tide. It’s just not going to lift all boats.”
— The rising tide that lifts some boats (Substack, Jan 2026)

> “That’s the work. It’s not easy, and nobody’s going to hand you a roadmap. But if you’ve read this far, you probably already knew that.”
— Why Your Productivity Initiatives Keep Disappointing (Substack, Feb 2026)

### Central metaphor carrying through every section

When a post has a strong conceptual frame, it should do work in every section — not just set up the intro. The metaphor earns its keep by recurring, not by being explained once.

> “That’s the physics of measurement in action” / “AI hasn’t overridden any of it” / “the same physics apply — but to a pipeline that looks nothing like the one we built our practices around”
— The Physics of Software Delivery (recurring through every section)

> “haves and have-nots” / “the window is narrowing” / “AI is a rising tide”
— The rising tide that lifts some boats (recurring through every section)

### Practical endings with actionable insight (archived — Swarmia blog voice)

These ending patterns are from the Swarmia advisory register. Do not use for Substack posts — see “Landing with uncertainty or a gut-punch” above.

> “Keep experimenting, learning, and having fun with it. Maybe you’ll figure out how to code faster *and* ship more.”
— Code faster, ship… the same? (Aug 2025)

> “So just start. Experiment first, adopt intentionally, measure where it makes sense, then answer the cost question. These tools are promising, and with the right approach, they can genuinely improve how your teams work.”
— A staged approach to AI adoption (Dec 2025)

### Clear structure with meaningful section headers (Swarmia register, use selectively)

Headers should mark genuine section breaks, not SEO scaffolding. When used, they should be conversational fragments or direct questions, not stage-named outlines like “Stage One: Experiment.” The Substack and practitioner registers use fewer headers and let the prose carry the structure.

### Bulleted lists (use sparingly, Swarmia register)

Bullets are appropriate for genuinely enumerable things — a real list of steps, a real list of tools. They are not a substitute for prose argumentation. In the Substack and practitioner registers, the prose does the argumentative work. If you find yourself bulleting the main argument, rewrite it as prose.

## What NOT To Do

### No invented preambles
- ❌ “I’ve been thinking about this topic for months…”
- ❌ “This is something I’ve wanted to write about for a long time…”
- ❌ “Let me take you on a journey…”
- ❌ “In today’s rapidly changing world…”

### No empty transitions
- ❌ “Let’s dive in.”
- ❌ “With that said…”
- ❌ “Now, let’s explore…”
- ❌ “Without further ado…”
- ❌ “So, what does this all mean?”

### No flowery language
- ❌ “In the ever-evolving landscape of…”
- ❌ “As we navigate these turbulent waters…”
- ❌ “On this journey of discovery…”
- ❌ “The tapestry of modern software development…”

### No generic platitudes
- ❌ “Change is the only constant.”
- ❌ “The future is uncertain but exciting.”
- ❌ “Technology continues to reshape our world.”
- ❌ “At the end of the day…”

### No summary conclusions that just repeat
- ❌ “In conclusion, we’ve seen that…”
- ❌ “To summarize the key points…”
- ❌ “As we’ve discussed throughout this piece…”
- ❌ “Let’s recap what we’ve learned…”

### No telegraphing insights
Don’t announce that you’re about to say something important — just say it. Phrases like “Here’s the thing:”, “The thing is:”, “Here’s the nuance:”, “Here’s what I’ve realized:” all signal “important point incoming” without actually making the point. Cut the windup and deliver the pitch.

### Challenges over questions of willingness
Prefer “the challenge is to actually do X” over “the question is whether you’re willing to do X.” The first frames the work ahead; the second frames the reader’s character. One is actionable, the other is slightly accusatory.

### Engage counterarguments from experience
Don’t just acknowledge counterarguments and move on. Add a personal take, a parenthetical pushback, something from lived experience. The counterargument paragraph is where voice shows most clearly. “There’s a fair counterargument that X” followed immediately by “But anyway…” wastes the opportunity. Instead: “There’s a fair counterargument that X. (Even with that argument in mind, I still struggle to think of…)” — then move on.

### Complete abstract concepts
Push one step further than the label. “Psychological safety” is a term; “confidence that raising the issue might actually lead to something” is what it means in practice. Don’t stop at the abstraction — ground it in what people actually experience.

### Trim intensifiers by default
“A lot more” → “more”. “Really” rarely adds meaning. “Very” is almost always cuttable. When in doubt, remove the modifier and see if the sentence loses anything. Usually it doesn’t.

### Soften direct reader criticism
“Look in the mirror” reads as a lecture. “Take a peek in the mirror” keeps the point, loses the edge. When addressing readers directly about their shortcomings, a lighter touch lands better than finger-wagging.

### Avoid overgeneralizing groups
When describing a group of people (especially sympathetically), don’t make absolute claims that imply everyone failed in identical ways. Add qualifiers that acknowledge individual variation:
- ❌ “They didn’t learn how to scope work”
- ✅ “They didn’t *necessarily* learn how to scope work”
- ❌ “They didn’t develop judgment”
- ✅ “They didn’t *universally* develop judgment”

The argument is about systemic patterns, not universal individual failures.

### The reject/affirm family (banned)

These constructions sound like TED talks. They announce cleverness instead of delivering it. State the affirmative claim directly; the “not X” half is usually removable, and stripping it produces a stronger sentence.

The full family:
- ❌ “X is not Y. X is Z.” (two-sentence reject-then-affirm)
- ❌ “Not X, but Y.” (single-sentence conjunctive)
- ❌ “Not X. Y.” (fragment rejection)
- ❌ “Not because X, but because Y.” (causal version)
- ❌ “X doesn’t [verb] because A. X [verb]s because B.” (verb-negated causal)
- ❌ “The problem is not X. The problem is Y.” (named-subject)
- ❌ “X rather than Y” when Y carries the rhetorical emphasis
- ❌ “It’s not about X, it’s about Y.” (the TED-talk reframe)

If the contrast is essential to meaning, rework as clause-heavy prose without the reject/affirm scaffolding. Beware the trap: just cutting the rejection half often leaves a staccato fragment where a developed sentence should be — *reconstruct* rather than delete.

### Prose rhythm: flowing clauses over staccato

Default to flowing, clause-heavy prose. Staccato rhythm — parallel punchy declaratives, fragment lists, and short sentences stacked in sequence — reads as assertion without development. This is the most common AI tell in drafts and the hardest pattern to see while writing.

Patterns to avoid:
- ❌ **Parallel triples**: “Somebody X. Somebody Y. Somebody Z.” — the third repetition locks it in as staccato regardless of individual sentence strength. Join with semicolons or fold into one clause-heavy sentence.
- ❌ **Stakeholder enumeration triples**: “Your CFO wants X. Your board wants Y. Product wants Z.” — three parallel “your [stakeholder] wants” constructions in a row. This is a reliable AI tell. Fold into a single clause: “The factory-floor mental model shows up everywhere at the executive level — headcount-to-output ratios, metrics that look like manufacturing KPIs, timeline commitments that treat software like assembly.”
- ❌ **The punchy reversal**: “X hasn't changed. But Y has.” — the short second sentence as a deliberate contrast punch reads as AI reaching for drama. Recast as a single flowing clause: “The same physics apply, but to a pipeline that looks nothing like the one we built our practices around.”
- ❌ **Fragment lists as sentences**: “Compliance obligations, on-call rotations, a dependency graph nobody fully understands.” — belongs inside a sentence with em-dashes or a colon, not as its own paragraph-level beat.
- ❌ **Five short declaratives in a row**: each one may be sharp, but stacked they become noise. One punchy sentence in a section lands; five become a hammer.
- ❌ **Identical rhythm across a paragraph**: if every sentence has the same length, or every sentence starts with the same word, the paragraph has a rhythm problem even when each sentence is individually fine.

A short sentence is a tool, not a default. Use it deliberately, one or two per section, in places where the rhythm shift lands a point. Default to clauses.

### Presumptive scene-setting

Don't open a scene by telling the reader they've been somewhere or felt something. "You've been in this meeting" announces the scene instead of creating it — it reaches for false intimacy rather than earning it, and it's one of the most common AI tells.

- ❌ "You've been in this meeting."
- ❌ "You know this feeling."
- ❌ "You've seen this happen before."

Describe the scene directly and let the reader recognize themselves:
- ✅ "The CEO bought AI coding tools six months ago and wants to know why the team isn't shipping faster."
- ✅ "A team that's 100% allocated to feature work and somehow still slow — you probably already know why."

### No filler paragraphs
- ❌ Paragraphs that restate what was just said in different words
- ❌ Paragraphs that preview what’s coming without adding substance
- ❌ Paragraphs that exist only to create “flow”
- ❌ Paragraphs of generic context (“In recent years, AI has…”)

## Structure Patterns

- **Headers break up content** with meaningful labels, not cute ones
- **Short to medium paragraphs** — rarely more than 4-5 sentences
- **Lists when appropriate** — but not as a crutch to avoid writing prose
- **Links where they add value** — 3-5 per 1000 words max, woven naturally into sentences
- **Footnotes for asides** — used sparingly for tangential but interesting points

## Personal Memoir & Reflection Voice

The memoir mode uses different techniques than professional writing. In the professional context, this mode surfaces in posts like “A personal AI snapshot” and “Girl & Computer” — where personal trajectory intersects with technical or industry observation.

### Concrete specificity grounds abstract reflection

> “Back then, my dad made eyeglasses. My mom stayed at home with me and my year-old sister — which she’d continue to do until I was a teenager, when my brother finally entered kindergarten in 1990 or so. Their mortgage in 1982 was $79 ($219 in 2021 dollars), which was a good thing because my dad made about $13,000 a year.”
— Girl & Computer (Medium, March 2021)

> “A cassette player nearly the size of a loaf of bread was our disk drive, and we had to set the volume *just* right in order to read anything off a cassette tape.”
— Girl & Computer

### Place-grounding with self-aware asides

> “We lived in Weedsport, New York, a small town in the middle of nowhere and about an hour’s drive north of Cornell University (a place, I’d later learn, that lots of people have heard of, fairly few of whom can point to it on a map).”
— Girl & Computer

### The gut-punch ending

> “My gut is punched, viscerally, by the remarkable impact of the choices that were made, the opportunities that were offered, the doors that were held open, the $99.95 that was spent now decades ago.”
— Girl & Computer

## Professional Advice Voice (Additional Examples)

The professional advice mode shares core principles with memoir writing — directness, specificity, dry wit — but applies them differently.

### Opening that establishes authority through insight, not credentials

> “Speaking at conferences has changed the course of my life. Conferences are where I’ve met some of my best friends; they’ve taken me across the country and around the world; they’ve given me access to a network of people that means I’m never wanting for work or for guidance.”
— Speaker Notes (Medium, June 2013)

## Characteristic Phrases & Patterns

These recurring patterns appear across writing modes:

- “if we’re being honest” / “if we’re being quite honest” — for admitting uncomfortable truths
- “let’s say” — understatement before something stronger
- “ask me how I know!” — self-deprecating parenthetical
- “uh,” — for self-aware pauses (“uh, increase the velocity and quality of frontend development”)
- “just like,” — casual dismissal
- “very-not-large!” / “less-than-stellar” — hyphenated compounds for dry emphasis
- Italics for *emphasis* on key words, especially in memoir mode
- Em dashes for asides — always with spaces on both sides (` — ` not `—`)
- Parenthetical qualifiers that add authenticity rather than hedging
- **Alliteration** — used deliberately for rhythm and memorability. Especially in headers, section titles, and closing lines. Not forced, but actively sought when a phrase can land it naturally.
